Source Taxonomy & Names
Ayurvedic names
Mandukaparni; Manduki. The text explains the name in relation to the frog-like leaf form and/or its occurrence near water and spreading habit.
Botanical name
Hydrocotyle asiatica — the name printed in the supplied P. V. Sharma text.
Modern taxonomy
Hydrocotyle asiatica is a synonym of the accepted name Centella asiatica (L.) Urb. This modern taxonomic note is separate from the historical name used in the source.
Morphology & Identification — translated from P. V. Sharma
Habit
A creeping, spreading herb that grows along the ground and may persist for two to three years.
Stem
Roots, leaves, flowers and fruits arise from the nodes of the stem.
Leaves
Alternate, kidney-shaped leaves, approximately 1–2½ inches across, with seven veins. Fresh leaves are described as bitter and slightly aromatic; the dried leaves lose much of their taste and aroma.
Flowers
Small, bluish-white to reddish flowers, appearing in the spring season according to the source.
Fruit
Small fruits, occurring together in clusters, generally about 2–7 in number.
Root
Slender and thread-like.
Distribution
Reported from India and Sri Lanka, especially along the margins of water bodies during the rainy season.
Chemical Composition — source translation
Reported constituent
The text reports an alkaloidal constituent called “Brahmin” and states that the leaves contain about 78% water together with some volatile oil.
Ash
The text reports about 12.5% ash in the dried leaves, containing resin, fatty/aromatic matter, gum, sugar, albumin and salts.
Dravyaguna Properties — P. V. Sharma
Rasa
Tikta — bitter
Anurasa
Kashaya–Madhura — astringent–sweet
Guna
Laghu, Sara — light, flowing/mobilising
Virya
Shita — cooling
Vipaka
Madhura — sweet
Prabhava
Medhya — intellect/cognition-supporting action
Doshakarma
Tridosha-shamaka, with particular pacifying action on Kapha and Pitta.
System-wise Actions — translated from the source
External
Application to the skin is described as promoting blood circulation.
Nervous system
Medhya; described as improving memory and particularly the retention capacity of the brain.
Digestive
Its bitter taste is described as stimulating digestion; its astringent and cooling qualities also give a stambhana action.
Cardiovascular
Described as Hridya and useful for reducing swelling.
Respiratory
Promotes Kapha expulsion and helps clear the voice.
Urinary
Cooling potency is described as diuretic.
Reproductive
Sweet post-digestive effect and cooling potency are described as galactagogue.
Skin & wounds
Promotes cutaneous blood circulation and is described as useful in various skin disorders; also used for cleansing and healing wounds.
Fever
Described as aiding digestion of Ama and as antipyretic.
General
Sweet Vipaka and cooling potency are described as Balya and Vayasthapana, supporting general strength and longevity.
Therapeutic Uses — translated from P. V. Sharma
Nervous
Used in brain weakness and associated unmada and apasmara, based on its Medhya and memory-promoting actions.
Digestive
Agnimandya, Atisara and Grahani.
Cardiovascular / swelling
Cardiac weakness and swelling; the text also mentions Gandamala, Shlipada and other swellings.
Respiratory
Kasa, Shwasa and Svarabheda.
Urinary
Mutrakrichra and, when required, Prameha.
Lactation
Used after delivery when there is reduced breast milk production.
Skin
Kushtha, chronic wounds and wasting-related wounds; the text also describes use in the second stage of Phiranga when the disorder is established in skin and tissues.
Fever / Ama
Ama disorders and fevers associated with Ama.
General debility
Used for general weakness on account of its Vayasthapana and Balya actions.
Part Used & Dose — source translation
Part used
Panchanga — the whole plant.
Dose
Svarasa: 1–2 tola; root powder: 3–12 ratti; Panchanga powder: 3–5 masha. These historical units are reproduced from the source and are not modernised here.
Clinical caution: This is a historical textual dose statement from the supplied book, not a modern prescribing recommendation. Do not convert or apply it clinically without expert review and appropriate contemporary standards.
Specific Classical Preparations
Preparations named
Brahmi-paka, Brahmi-panaka, Saraswatarishta, Saraswata-ghrita, Brahmi-taila, Brahmi-sattva and Brahmi-malhara.
Adverse effects
The source states that excessive use may produce cold-related Vata aggravation with dullness, headache, tremor and depression, with redness and itching of the skin. It advises reducing or stopping use.
Management
The text recommends Virechana and other Vata-pacifying measures, particularly dry coriander, for managing the described adverse effects.
Processing note
If drying is necessary, the source recommends shade-drying because sun-drying removes volatile oil and reduces potency. It therefore states that a decoction or infusion is not prepared from the drug.
Classical Textual Notes
The supplied pages quote classical authorities including Sushruta, Bhavaprakasha and Charaka. In summary, the cited passages describe Mandukaparni as cooling, bitter, light, Medhya and Rasayana, with uses extending to memory, skin disorders, fever, cough, Prameha, swelling and other conditions. The page also cites a Sushruta passage describing its use as a fresh juice and a Charaka passage in the context of Medhya/Rasayana therapy.
