Morphology

The source describes an annual herb about 3–4 feet high. The stem is green, soft, hairy and somewhat succulent. Leaves are long, broad, alternate and sessile, with a lobed margin. Flowers are described as white, red or dark-coloured. The fruit is a rounded capsule, described as resembling a small pomegranate and known as Doda; the seed is white or black, sweet and unctuous, known as poppy seed or Khashkhash.

The book records three seed/flower colour forms and also notes geographical varieties. It gives distribution in temperate regions including Asia Minor, Persia, China, Nepal and India, with several Indian regions specifically mentioned.

Chemical Composition — Source Summary

The source describes opium as containing numerous alkaloids and other constituents, including morphine, codeine, thebaine, narcotine, narceine and papaverine, together with secondary alkaloids and organic acids such as meconic acid. It notes that morphine is chiefly associated with analgesic action and that Indian and Turkish opium are described as differing in relative effects.

System-wise Karma

Nervous system: The source describes Vedanasthapana, Nidrajanana and Akshepa-hara actions and discusses its use in pain, insomnia and convulsive conditions.

Digestive system: It is described as reducing salivation, producing astringent/stambhana effects and relieving abdominal pain; the source also discusses its use in selected diarrhoeal and febrile conditions.

Cardiovascular system: The source describes slowing of heart rate with maintained or increased cardiac strength and a haemostatic effect.

Respiratory system: It is described as Vata-Kapha shamaka and is discussed in cough, dyspnoea and inflammatory respiratory conditions.

Urinary system: The source notes a reducing effect on urinary sugar and mentions use in Ikshumeha.

Reproductive system: The source describes seminal stambhana and mentions use in premature ejaculation and selected obstetric/post-partum pain contexts.

Skin and temperature: Its Ushna quality is described as useful in cold-related complaints; the source also discusses antipyretic use.

Therapeutic Applications
  • Painful conditions and selected neuralgic pain states
  • Insomnia and selected convulsive disorders
  • Abdominal pain and selected diarrhoeal conditions
  • Selected bleeding conditions, according to the source
  • Selected cough and dyspnoea conditions
  • Selected urinary and reproductive indications described in the source
  • Selected febrile and cold-related conditions

The book explicitly states that Ahiphena is not described in the classical Samhitas and notes its description in Rajanighantu and Bhavaprakasha.

Safety & Restrictions
Ahiphena is a controlled narcotic source. This monograph is for botanical, Ayurvedic and educational identification only. The source discusses toxicity, dependence, contraindications and the need for caution in children, older adults and chronic illness. No preparation or extraction procedure is provided here.
